PROTOCOL 7 · THE CONTINUANCE FILES · M05

The Last Water Map

GM Field Guide r001 · Petra, 363 CE · Level 5 · 4–5 hours

Run This Tonight — The Whole Adventure in Two Minutes

A major earthquake has struck Petra. Aletheia sends the Vectors to preserve what appears to be the last useful plan of the city's extraordinary water network. The map is real and valuable—but the quake has already made parts of it wrong.

Petra survives through channels, pipes, cisterns, reservoirs, diversion works, and human maintenance knowledge. No surviving person understands all of it. The true Carrier is the city's distributed ability to reconstruct, teach, and operate its damaged water system.

The mission begins as a search for an object, becomes an exploration of a wounded city, and ends as civic triage. The Covenant argues that making infrastructure legible and interoperable is precisely the tendency that eventually enables Aletheia's centralized power.

GM priority: Players must understand Petra through play. Never require ancient-hydraulics knowledge. Give visible problems, competing human needs, and comprehensible choices.

Arrival — Make Petra Real

The stone is the first thing that seems impossible.

Cliffs the color of rust, rose and old blood rise on both sides of you, cut by a narrow road that twists between them. Human hands have carved channels along the rock itself. Here and there clay pipe disappears into masonry or beneath paving. Above, pieces of stone still tumble after the earthquake, striking the canyon floor with sounds like distant doors slamming.

Then the road opens and the city appears.

Columns lean at wrong angles. Walls have opened into rooms. Dust hangs in the sunlight until people moving through it become silhouettes. A donkey screams somewhere ahead. Men and women carry baskets, timber, blankets—and jars. Everywhere, jars.

Some are full enough to require two hands.

Most are empty.

Level 5 pressureSeveral legitimate emergencies occur at once; veteran Vectors cannot personally solve all of them.
Path revelationThe seven-stroke pattern aligns with relationships among independent continuity nodes.

Cast

Samira bat Malik: practical water steward who wants one authoritative map until reality proves it insufficient.

Diodoros: injured conduit repairer who remembers undocumented routes and repairs.

Nadiya: neighborhood cistern organizer who knows which stores are usable and which people are already becoming sick.

Marcellus: civic record keeper protecting the Last Water Map for entirely reasonable reasons.

Covenant operative: will help restore immediate water access but opposes making the whole city's dependencies permanently legible.

1 — The City Cut From Stone

A damaged structure has trapped residents while a nearby channel spills precious water into rubble. The team cannot perfectly solve both without spending resources, exposing capabilities, or delegating.

Veteran-play principle: Let Level 5 Vectors be powerful. Challenge them with scope and simultaneity rather than simply higher target numbers.

2 — The Queue

You hear the water point before you see it: pottery knocking against pottery, sandals scraping stone, voices climbing over one another.

The line bends around a cracked wall. Children hold cups. A woman braces an amphora against her hip. At the front, water falls from a stone outlet in a stream no wider than your finger.

Beside it sits a second basin.

Dry.

Someone has tied a strip of cloth around its pipe. Nobody will drink from it.

Samira looks at the crowd, then at you. “Yesterday both of these ran.”

One source has stopped, another may be contaminated, and official records conflict with local experience.

3 — The Last Water Map

The sheet has been repaired so many times that it is almost cloth. Lines in several inks cross the surface: channels, cisterns, reservoirs, notes squeezed into margins by different hands. Some have faded brown. Others are fresh.

Marcellus lays both palms beside it.

“There,” he says. “The city remembers itself.”

For a few minutes, let the map feel like the answer. It provides three actionable routes. One will prove wrong.

4 — The First Contradiction

A mapped route no longer works. Exploration reveals an improvised repair or bypass absent from the plan.

The air changes first.

Outside, Petra is dust and sun. Here the stone sweats cold against your fingertips. Your light catches plaster smoothed over the channel walls and mineral lines left by water that once stood higher.

A ceramic pipe emerges from one wall, crosses where the map says there should be nothing, and disappears beneath a slab patched with different mortar.

Diodoros touches the repair and laughs once.

“Your map is older than this mistake.”

Failure never hides this clue; it costs time, position, equipment, injury or Exposure.

6 — Reconstruction

Bring the information together using annotations, tokens, or a simple node-and-line sketch. This is not a hydraulic-engineering puzzle.

Infrastructure-blind rule: State choices in ordinary language: “This cistern can supply these homes if this route is open.”

The reconstructed network is better than the original map because it includes observed conditions, disagreement, local knowledge and post-quake changes.

7 — Covenant Threshold

“Give the thirsty people water. Repair every channel you can reach. We will help you.”

The operative puts one finger on the growing web of annotations.

“But this is not water. This is a city made readable. Who drinks where. Which district depends on which reservoir. Which failure forces which people to move.”

“You know what eventually learns to read maps like this.”

The Covenant proposes using the reconstruction during the emergency and then preventing broad copying. Sympathy with that argument is a valid player position.

8 — City-Scale Crisis

Trigger an aftershock, collapse, contamination discovery or blocked outlet. At least two districts cannot receive the easiest solution simultaneously.

The aftershock begins as a vibration beneath your feet.

Then the city answers it.

Dust pours from the cliffs. A column falls somewhere beyond the street. From below comes a deeper sound—a rushing hiss where there should be silence.

Water spills across the paving stones.

For one absurd second everyone simply watches it run downhill.

Then the crowd moves.

If combat occurs, it must protect an objective rather than merely defeat enemies.

9 — Propagation

Players decide who can independently preserve and teach the reconstructed system. Fragile: one map/expert remains indispensable. Established: several nodes can reconstruct essentials. Durable: overlapping knowledge survives loss of any one node. Durable success usually creates more historical footprint.

10 — Verification

Aletheia does not display the map.

She removes it.

What remains are names, districts, cisterns, channels, memories and copies—points of light connected by thin lines.

One line appears. Then another. Then five more.

The shape is familiar before you understand why.

Seven strokes.

Not drawn on the network.

Made by it.

Failure = Triage

A failed roll may mean one district waits longer, one source is lost, one NPC is injured, one route requires future technology, one information node disappears, or the Vectors must choose which problem remains unresolved. Never erase the only path forward.

Historical Guardrails

The Last Water Map and named mission NPCs are fictional. Do not imply Petra was abandoned in 363. Do not make the Vectors invent Nabataean hydraulic engineering. Ground the types of infrastructure and damage in archaeology while leaving uncertain local relationships fictional.

Level 5 / Advancement Test

Record whether players use broader VAM options, improved Skills/Abilities, HP resilience, and tactical flexibility. If Level 5 feels mechanically identical to Level 1, flag it for the post-path advancement audit rather than inflating difficulty.

Debrief

Who owned the water knowledge? Did making the city more resilient also make it more governable? What did the team choose not to preserve? Does the seven-stroke pattern represent a person, an intelligence, a rule—or the shape of continuity itself?
