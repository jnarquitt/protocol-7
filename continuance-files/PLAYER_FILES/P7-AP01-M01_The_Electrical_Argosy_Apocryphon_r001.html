To Our Adventurous Readers

M

AN has crossed the ocean, pierced the mountain, chained the lightning, and caused his whispered word to outrun the swiftest locomotive. Yet every triumph makes a new dependency. A city fed by one pipe is a city awaiting thirst. A nation instructed by one library has placed a match beneath its own memory. An empire governed from one chamber has already designed the instrument of its capture.

In this number Mr. Erasmus Vale continues his celebrated romance of the vegetable comet and the subterranean refuge. Some correspondents object that Miss Miranda Quell behaves with an independence unbecoming either her sex or her station. We reply that catastrophe is a famously indifferent chaperone.

Readers are invited to study the enclosed plate. Seven gentlemen of engineering have separately assured us that its arrangement is impossible. Curiously, each supplied the same correction.

Blue-pencil library notation: “Six deny the route. The seventh denies the map.”

What Has Gone Before

In the year 2006, Professor Septimus Rook detects beyond Jupiter a ribbon of living black vegetation traveling without root, soil, or vessel. He calculates that it will brush the Earth and cast innumerable spores into its atmosphere.

The governments of Europe and America commission the Imperial Sanctuary, a prodigious city beneath Mount Resolute. Its single gate can be sealed against poison. Its central engine will pump air and water to fifty thousand souls. The governors praise its unity.

Miss Miranda Quell, a draughtswoman employed by the Resolute Works, discovers that the Sanctuary’s strength is also its fatal weakness. One gate may be blocked. One engine may fail. One archive may burn. One governor may become a tyrant. She proposes seven independent routes for every necessity.

The Council dismisses her. Dr. Silvanus Vent, philosopher and former architect of the Works, secretly offers to save Quell if she destroys her drawings. He believes a perfect refuge would create a perfect despotism.

At the close of Part III, the Earth passes through the comet’s leading dust. Saint Aurelia shakes. Fires begin along the waterfront. Quell carries a blue ledger containing the only complete record of which experimental structures endured the first shock.

The questions with which Part III concluded:
Is Quell herself indispensable—or only her knowledge? Can a plan remain free after it enters an institution? Why does the sealed seventh route appear on drawings made before Quell was born?

The City Beneath the Cinders

Part IV — The Three Copies

By Erasmus Vale, Fellow of the Royal Astronomical Society

I. The Falling Hour

A

T twelve minutes after five, the clocks of Saint Aurelia agreed for the final time.

The pavement moved east while the houses appeared to move west. A stone angel left the Exchange tower, retained for one astonished instant the attitude of flight, and broke upon the street among a rain of glass. Horses sat upon their haunches like dogs. Telegraph wires struck blue fire from one another. Beneath those wires Miranda Quell continued counting.

“Six bays,” she said, marking the blue ledger. “The iron brace holds. The adjoining brick has opened at the third course. Ground wet beneath both.”

A policeman seized her sleeve. “Madam, the city is falling.”

“Then this is the only morning on which it may be measured.”

She freed herself and crossed toward the insurance warehouses. Behind her came a second convulsion, less violent but more intimate, as though some enormous buried animal had adjusted its shoulder beneath the town.

At the corner, a child stood beneath a striped awning whose iron frame had folded around him. A gray-coated stranger reached the boy first. The stranger wore no badge, yet gave orders with such quiet certainty that six men obeyed before remembering to question him. Together they lifted the frame.

Quell looked once at the stranger’s hands. They bore no soot.

Then the power lines flashed, smoke swallowed the corner, and the stranger was gone.

Margin notation: “A clean hand is dirtier than a burned one.”

II. What a Brick Remembers

By six o’clock the fire had acquired a geography. It traveled faster where broken mains left the hydrants dumb. It turned where stone walls held. It leapt where frightened men used powder without understanding the wind.

Quell reached the temporary office of the Aurelia Mutual Assurance Company. Its chief engineer, Mr. Pender, stood in the doorway guarding cabinets whose contents were already burning.

“Save the policies,” he commanded.

“Policies describe value,” Quell replied. “My sheets describe survival.”

“Your sheets are observations made by a draftsman.”

“Then copy them under an engineer’s name. The buildings will not object.”

She laid out twelve comparisons. Brick upon dry rock had cracked but remained upright. Brick upon wet fill had opened and fallen. Iron frames with flexible joints had swayed and returned. Rigid pipes had broken where independent couplings endured. Two houses identical above the street had behaved as different creatures because the earth beneath them differed.

Pender’s disdain weakened into arithmetic.

“Where did you learn this method?” he asked.

Quell could not answer. The columns had occurred to her months before in a dream of a circular chamber beneath a red sky. Six corridors entered the chamber. A seventh stopped at the edge of the page.

“It does not matter where the method began,” she said. “It matters whether another observer can repeat it.”

A wall collapsed across the street. Pender turned. When he looked back, three carbon leaves were missing from Quell’s stack.

III. Dr. Vent’s Bargain

Dr. Silvanus Vent found her at the freight platform, writing as sparks settled on her coat.

“I can take you below the military reservoir,” he said. “The chamber will survive the fires.”

“Will my ledger?”

“No.”

Vent did not threaten her. That made him more dangerous.

“The Imperial Sanctuary will save fifty thousand,” he said. “It will also decide which fifty thousand. Its engine will provide every breath, and therefore its governor will own every breath. Your seven routes would make the system stronger. They would make that authority permanent.”

“A second pipe is not a philosophy.”

“Every pipe is a philosophy. It declares who may close the valve.”

He opened his coat. Inside hung a brass cylinder no larger than a cigar case. Its glass window displayed seven strokes converging upon a dark center.

“Destroy the ledger,” Vent said. “Live long enough to oppose what they will build from it.”

Quell studied him. “You have mistaken the object for the idea.”

“Have I?”

“The first copy crossed the bay before dawn.”

It was a lie. Vent’s eyes told her it was the correct lie.

From the smoke behind him came the gray-coated stranger and a woman dressed as a telegraph mechanic. Neither seemed surprised by the brass cylinder.

“Doctor,” said the mechanic, “ask her about the third copy.”

IV. The Little Giant

The first fire company they met had water enough to wet its own wheels and no more. Captain Rusk struck the nearest hydrant with a wrench as though punishment might restore its pressure.

Quell opened her ledger. Between structural comparisons she had recorded the peculiar behavior of the water mains. Most large lines had failed at rigid junctions. One narrow branch remained isolated by a forgotten valve.

“Twentieth and Church,” she said. “A little main. Separate pressure.”

Rusk laughed. “The little giant? That toy feeds a drinking trough.”

“Then let the horse drink after the city.”

The telegraph mechanic tore a street map from inside her coat and drew a blue circle around the junction. Her left glove had burned through at the thumb. She gave the map to a runner, leaving herself no guide through the smoke.

“If this is wrong—” Captain Rusk began.

“Then you will have lost a runner,” said Quell. “If it is right, the fire will learn where the city ends.”

The boy vanished south with the map.

Dr. Vent watched him go. “You preserve every instrument that will one day be used against you.”

“No,” Quell said. “I preserve the possibility that no single instrument will be enough.”

Far beyond sight, a hydrant coughed rust and then water.

V. The Ledger Crosses the Bay

The ferry slips were crowded with people carrying cages, clocks, portraits, ledgers, bedding, and children. Quell could not cross. No ferry captain would accept a crate of insurance papers while families remained on the wharf.

“The story says the ledger crosses twice without a ferry,” said the gray-coated stranger.

Quell stared at him. “What story?”

He did not answer.

The telegraph cables beneath the bay had not all failed. A clerk could transmit figures, but not drawings. A newspaper office could typeset conclusions, but not preserve raw evidence. A photographer could send plates by military launch, but a plate could not teach another engineer how to repeat a comparison.

Quell divided the method.

The first copy became numbers: twelve paired observations telegraphed to Professor Haldane in Oakland.

The second became language: a printer’s proof explaining that structures must be compared by construction, foundation, ground, utility connections, and failure sequence.

The third remained the blue ledger, entrusted to a person Quell did not know.

“Now none is complete,” said Vent.

“Now no fire is complete either.”

At the telegraph desk, seven men read the incoming table. Six argued over whether Saint Aurelia had fallen because its buildings were weak or because its earth was treacherous. The seventh man silently turned the page and asked why the neighboring structures had stood.

When he lifted his hand, the seven-stroke figure remained beneath it in printer’s ink.

VI. Beneath the Cinders

Night found Quell, Vent, and the strangers beneath the south arcade of the Ferry Building. The clock above them showed twenty minutes before midnight. Refugees moved through the smoke in a continuous human river.

Vent held the brass cylinder.

“The comet has not yet arrived,” Quell said. “Why does your instrument bear the sign from my dream?”

“Because your dream is a memory,” he replied.

“Of what?”

“Of instructions older than every city that has misunderstood them.”

The ground shook. For one instant the arcade’s pillars seemed to extend downward through layers of other architecture: brick vaults, fitted stone, a chamber cut into living rock, a circle of upright pillars beneath an unfamiliar sky. At the center of every layer stood the same dark junction with six open routes and one sealed way.

Quell saw men in skins measuring stars. Priests carrying water through tunnels. Surveyors burying copper plates. Children singing numbers whose meaning their teachers had forgotten. Engineers drawing a refuge beneath a mountain. The acts were separated by ages, yet each hand completed a line begun by another.

“Who designed the Sanctuary?” she whispered.

“Everyone who did not know what they were building,” said Vent.

The gray-coated stranger looked toward the clock. “We have to go.”

“Where?”

But white light divided the crowd, and the strangers vanished from history so neatly that the people nearest them remembered only smoke.

Quell looked down. The blue ledger was gone.

In her hand remained a single page bearing six signatures and a blank seventh line.

Practical Correspondence

ONE ENGINE IS MORE EFFICIENT

Sir—Mr. Vale’s sevenfold Sanctuary is wasteful fancy. Any competent engineer knows that duplicated engines require duplicated attendants, stores, and expense. The public is best served by one powerful plant under disciplined management.

—S.V., Resolute, Colorado

WHO SELECTS THE SAVED?

Sir—Your romance avoids its indecent question. If fifty thousand may enter and five million must remain outside, what instrument selects them? Wealth? Usefulness? Obedience? Chance? A refuge is merely a ballot printed in concrete.

—A Custodian, Boston

SEVEN IDENTICAL CORRECTIONS

Sir—Your diagram miscounts its routes. There are six paths and a central chamber, not seven paths. I enclose the corrected figure.

—H. R., Baltimore

The office received this letter seven times, in seven hands, from seven cities. Each enclosed correction was identical down to an accidental ink blot. One correspondent’s postmark is dated October 18, 2005.

THE VEGETABLE COMET

Sir—Living matter could not traverse the cold between planets. Yet if spores traveled within a continuous wet medium rather than empty space, might Jupiter provide the first garden and Earth the last?

—A Young Naturalist, Woking
